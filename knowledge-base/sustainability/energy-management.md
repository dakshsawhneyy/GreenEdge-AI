# Energy Management Best Practices

## Executive Summary

Energy management is a systematic approach to optimizing energy consumption in commercial and industrial buildings. This comprehensive guide provides actionable strategies for reducing energy costs, improving operational efficiency, and achieving sustainability goals. Effective energy management can reduce operating costs by 15-30% while significantly decreasing carbon emissions.

## 1. Understanding Energy Consumption Patterns

### Peak Load Management

Peak load represents the maximum energy demand during a specific time period. Managing peak loads is critical because:

- Utility companies charge higher rates during peak demand periods (demand charges)
- Peak demand can account for 30-70% of commercial electricity bills
- Reducing peak load by just 10-15% can yield significant cost savings
- Grid strain during peaks increases the risk of brownouts and equipment stress

**Strategies for Peak Load Reduction:**

1. **Load Shifting**: Move energy-intensive operations to off-peak hours (typically nights and weekends)
2. **Load Shedding**: Temporarily reduce or turn off non-critical loads during peak periods
3. **Energy Storage**: Use battery systems to store off-peak energy for use during peak times
4. **Demand Response Programs**: Participate in utility programs that provide incentives for reducing demand

### Baseline Energy Consumption

Establishing an accurate baseline is essential for measuring improvement. A proper baseline should:

- Cover at least 12 months of data to account for seasonal variations
- Be normalized for weather conditions, occupancy changes, and operational variations
- Identify energy use by system (HVAC, lighting, plug loads, process equipment)
- Include both electricity and thermal energy (natural gas, steam, chilled water)

**Energy Use Breakdown in Typical Commercial Buildings:**
- HVAC Systems: 40-60% of total energy
- Lighting: 15-25%
- Plug Loads (computers, equipment): 10-20%
- Elevators and Motors: 5-10%
- Domestic Hot Water: 5-10%

## 2. HVAC Optimization Strategies

### Temperature Setpoint Optimization

HVAC systems are the largest energy consumers in most buildings. Proper temperature management can reduce HVAC energy consumption by 10-30%:

**Heating Season Recommendations:**
- Occupied hours: 68-70°F (20-21°C)
- Unoccupied hours: 60-65°F (15-18°C)
- Each 1°F reduction in heating setpoint saves approximately 3% on heating costs

**Cooling Season Recommendations:**
- Occupied hours: 74-76°F (23-24°C)
- Unoccupied hours: 80-85°F (27-29°C)
- Each 1°F increase in cooling setpoint saves approximately 3% on cooling costs

**Setback and Setup Strategies:**
- Implement nighttime temperature setbacks (heating) and setups (cooling)
- Start setback 1-2 hours before building closure
- Use smart recovery to reach comfortable temperatures before occupancy
- Consider outdoor temperature when scheduling recovery times

### Air Handling Unit (AHU) Optimization

**Variable Air Volume (VAV) Systems:**
- Reduce static pressure setpoints to minimum required levels (typically 1.0-1.5" water column)
- Reset supply air temperature based on outdoor conditions or zone demand
- Optimize minimum airflow setpoints (typically 30-50% of design flow)
- Implement demand-controlled ventilation using CO2 sensors

**Economizer Operations:**
- Free cooling can provide 100% of cooling needs when outdoor conditions are favorable
- Ensure economizers are functional and properly calibrated
- Typical economizer savings: 10-30% of annual cooling energy
- Enable economizers when outdoor temperature is below 55-65°F depending on humidity

**Fan Schedule Optimization:**
- Start fans 30-60 minutes before occupancy rather than hours in advance
- Implement occupied/unoccupied schedules tied to actual building use
- Use temporary occupancy overrides rather than continuous operation
- Shut down fans within 30 minutes of building vacancy

### Chiller Plant Optimization

Chillers can account for 50-70% of HVAC electricity consumption during cooling seasons:

**Chilled Water Temperature Reset:**
- Increase chilled water supply temperature from typical 42°F to 44-48°F when possible
- Each 1°F increase in chilled water temperature reduces chiller energy by 1-2%
- Monitor leaving chilled water temperature and zone valve positions
- Reset based on warmest zone demand or outdoor temperature

**Condenser Water Temperature Optimization:**
- Lower condenser water temperature when possible using cooling tower fans
- Each 1°F reduction in condenser water temperature reduces chiller energy by 1.5-2%
- Balance cooling tower fan energy against chiller energy savings
- Optimal condenser water temperature is typically 5-10°F above wet bulb temperature

**Sequencing Multiple Chillers:**
- Operate the most efficient chiller (highest kW/ton) first
- Stage chillers based on actual load rather than fixed setpoints
- Avoid operating chillers below 30-40% capacity where efficiency drops
- Consider running multiple chillers at higher part-load ratios rather than one at low load

### Boiler System Optimization

**Boiler Sequencing and Staging:**
- Operate the most efficient boiler first (highest combustion efficiency)
- Stage boilers to maintain 60-80% firing rate where efficiency is highest
- Avoid continuous operation at low fire where efficiency degrades
- Lock out unnecessary boilers during low-load conditions

**Hot Water Temperature Reset:**
- Reset hot water supply temperature based on outdoor air temperature
- Typical reset schedule: 180°F at 0°F outdoor, 140°F at 60°F outdoor
- Each 10°F reduction in supply temperature saves approximately 2-3% in fuel
- Ensure hot water temperature meets heating load requirements at all zones

**Combustion Air Control:**
- Optimize excess air to 5-15% depending on fuel type
- Too much excess air wastes heat up the stack
- Too little excess air causes incomplete combustion and emissions
- Regular combustion analysis ensures optimal air-fuel ratio

## 3. Lighting System Optimization

### LED Conversion and Upgrades

LED lighting uses 50-75% less energy than traditional lighting technologies:

**Retrofit Priorities:**
1. **High-Use Areas**: Corridors, lobbies, 24/7 spaces (highest energy savings)
2. **Hard-to-Maintain Areas**: High ceilings, difficult access (reduced maintenance costs)
3. **High-Wattage Fixtures**: Metal halide, high-pressure sodium (greatest per-fixture savings)
4. **Exterior Lighting**: Parking lots, security lighting (long operating hours)

**LED Selection Criteria:**
- Color temperature: 3000-4000K for offices, 5000K for industrial
- Color rendering index (CRI): Minimum 80, preferably 90+ for detailed work
- Dimming compatibility: 0-10V or DALI for maximum flexibility
- Rated life: Minimum 50,000 hours (L70)
- Warranty: Minimum 5 years for quality assurance

### Lighting Controls and Automation

Lighting controls can reduce lighting energy consumption by 30-60%:

**Occupancy Sensors:**
- Offices and conference rooms: 20-40% savings
- Restrooms and storage areas: 30-60% savings
- Corridors and stairwells: 40-70% savings
- Recommended timeout: 15-20 minutes for offices, 5-10 minutes for restrooms

**Daylight Harvesting:**
- Automatically dim lights in response to available daylight
- Greatest savings in perimeter zones within 15 feet of windows
- Requires photosensors and continuous dimming ballasts/drivers
- Typical savings: 20-60% in daylit zones

**Scheduling and Time Clocks:**
- Automatic off during unoccupied hours
- Gradual dimming before closing to remind occupants
- Override switches for after-hours work (auto-off after 2-4 hours)
- Astronomical time clocks for exterior lighting (automatic seasonal adjustment)

**Task Lighting and Multi-Level Switching:**
- Provide task lighting for detailed work rather than over-illuminating entire spaces
- Use bi-level or tri-level switching to match lighting to occupancy and task needs
- De-lamp fixtures in over-lit areas (remove 1/3 to 1/2 of lamps)
- Recommended light levels: 30-50 footcandles for offices, 50-100 for detailed tasks

### Light Level Optimization

Many spaces are over-lit based on outdated standards:

**Recommended Illumination Levels (footcandles):**
- Office areas (general): 30-50 fc
- Office areas (detailed work): 50-100 fc
- Conference rooms: 30-50 fc
- Corridors and lobbies: 10-20 fc
- Stairwells: 10-20 fc
- Storage areas: 5-10 fc
- Parking garages: 5-10 fc
- Exterior walkways: 1-5 fc

## 4. Building Envelope and Insulation

### Thermal Envelope Assessment

The building envelope is the physical separator between the interior and exterior environments:

**Common Envelope Issues:**
- Air leakage through cracks, gaps, and penetrations (20-40% of heating/cooling load)
- Inadequate insulation in walls, roofs, and foundations
- Thermal bridges through structural elements
- Poor window performance (single-pane, deteriorated seals)

**Air Sealing Priorities:**
1. Mechanical penetrations through walls and roofs
2. Door and window perimeters
3. Expansion joints and construction gaps
4. Electrical and plumbing penetrations
5. Loading dock doors and vestibules

### Window and Glazing Upgrades

Windows can account for 25-50% of heating and cooling loads:

**Window Film Applications:**
- Reduces solar heat gain by 50-80%
- Payback period: 3-7 years in cooling-dominated climates
- Minimal impact on visible light transmission with modern films
- Can extend life of existing windows by 10-15 years

**Storm Windows and Secondary Glazing:**
- Reduces heat loss by 25-50% compared to single-pane windows
- Lower cost alternative to full window replacement
- Interior storm windows also reduce condensation
- Particularly effective for historic buildings where replacement is restricted

**Window Replacement Criteria:**
- U-factor (heat transfer): Target 0.30 or lower
- Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (SHGC): 0.25-0.40 depending on climate
- Visible light transmittance: 0.50 or higher for occupied spaces
- Low-E coatings: Essential for energy performance

### Roof and Insulation Improvements

**Cool Roof Technologies:**
- Reflects 65-90% of solar energy compared to 5-20% for traditional roofs
- Reduces roof surface temperatures by 50-60°F
- Lowers cooling loads by 10-30% in cooling-dominated climates
- Options include white single-ply membranes, coatings, and light-colored tiles

**Insulation Upgrades:**
- Roof/ceiling: R-30 to R-60 depending on climate zone
- Walls: R-13 to R-21 depending on climate zone and construction type
- Foundation/slab: R-10 to R-20 perimeter insulation
- Prioritize attics and roofs first (greatest impact per dollar invested)

## 5. Motor and Drive Optimization

### Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs)

Motors account for 60-70% of industrial electricity consumption:

**VFD Applications and Savings:**
- Pumps: 30-50% energy savings with variable loads
- Fans: 30-60% energy savings with variable loads
- Compressors: 20-35% energy savings
- Conveyors: 10-30% energy savings depending on load profile

**Affinity Laws for Pumps and Fans:**
- Flow is proportional to speed
- Pressure is proportional to speed squared
- Power is proportional to speed cubed
- Example: Reducing fan speed by 20% (to 80% of full speed) reduces energy by 49%

**VFD Selection and Implementation:**
- Size VFD to match motor horsepower
- Ensure motor is compatible with VFD operation (inverter-duty rated)
- Provide adequate cooling for VFD enclosures
- Consider harmonic filters for large VFD installations
- Implement bypass capability for critical applications

### Motor Replacement and Efficiency

**Premium Efficiency Motors:**
- NEMA Premium efficiency motors use 2-8% less energy than standard efficiency
- Greatest savings in motors that operate continuously or at high load factors
- Payback period: 1-3 years for continuously operating motors
- Priority for replacement: 24/7 operation, >10 HP, >60% load factor

**Motor Right-Sizing:**
- Oversized motors operate at lower efficiency and power factor
- Target motor loading: 75-100% of rated capacity for optimal efficiency
- Motors operating below 50% load should be evaluated for replacement
- Consider two-speed motors for variable load applications

### Compressed Air System Optimization

Compressed air is one of the most expensive forms of energy:

**Leak Detection and Repair:**
- Compressed air leaks waste 20-30% of compressed air in typical facilities
- A 1/4" leak at 100 PSI wastes $8,000-12,000 per year in electricity
- Quarterly ultrasonic leak detection programs are recommended
- Tag and track all leaks with repair priorities

**Pressure Reduction:**
- Lower system pressure by 2 PSI reduces compressor energy by approximately 1%
- Many systems operate at 110-120 PSI when 90-100 PSI is sufficient
- Use pressure regulators for applications requiring lower pressures
- Eliminate artificial demand created by excessive system pressure

**Compressor Sequencing:**
- Use variable-speed compressors as trim units
- Operate fixed-speed compressors as base load
- Avoid compressor loading/unloading cycles (high energy waste)
- Implement master controls for multiple compressor systems

## 6. Energy Monitoring and Analytics

### Metering and Submetering

**Submetering Strategy:**
- Meter major energy consumers separately (chillers, boilers, large processes)
- Target systems consuming >10% of total energy
- Real-time data enables immediate response to anomalies
- Sub-metering cost: $1,000-5,000 per point depending on complexity

**Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):**
- Energy Use Intensity (EUI): kBtu/sq.ft./year or kWh/sq.ft./year
- Energy Cost Index: $/sq.ft./year
- Peak Demand: kW and $/kW/month
- Power Factor: Target 0.95 or higher to avoid utility penalties
- Equipment Efficiency: kW/ton for chillers, combustion efficiency for boilers

### Fault Detection and Diagnostics (FDD)

Automated FDD systems identify operational issues in real-time:

**Common Faults Detected:**
- Simultaneous heating and cooling
- Outdoor air dampers stuck open or closed
- Failed sensors providing incorrect readings
- Equipment operating during unoccupied hours
- Excessive energy consumption for given weather conditions

**FDD Implementation Benefits:**
- Identifies faults within days rather than months or years
- Reduces energy waste by 10-20% on average
- Prioritizes maintenance activities based on energy impact
- Typical payback period: 1-3 years

### Energy Management Information Systems (EMIS)

**EMIS Capabilities:**
- Centralized monitoring of all building systems and meters
- Automated fault detection and alerting
- Benchmarking against historical performance and similar buildings
- Integration with building automation systems (BAS)
- Energy forecasting and budget management

**EMIS Selection Criteria:**
- Cloud-based vs. on-premise deployment
- Integration capabilities with existing systems
- Reporting and visualization tools
- Mobile access for facility staff
- Scalability for future expansion

## 7. Renewable Energy Integration

### Solar Photovoltaic (PV) Systems

**System Sizing and Economics:**
- Commercial PV costs: $2.50-4.00 per watt installed
- Typical payback period: 5-10 years depending on incentives and electricity rates
- Federal investment tax credit: 30% through 2032
- System life: 25-30 years with 0.5% annual degradation

**Optimal Solar Applications:**
- Buildings with high daytime electrical loads
- Facilities with suitable roof area and minimal shading
- Locations with high electricity rates (>$0.12/kWh)
- Organizations with long-term facility ownership

**PV Performance Factors:**
- System orientation: South-facing optimal in Northern Hemisphere
- Tilt angle: Approximately equal to site latitude for year-round production
- Shading analysis: Critical for accurate production estimates
- Temperature coefficient: Panels lose 0.3-0.5% efficiency per °C above 25°C

### Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS)

**Storage System Applications:**
- Peak demand shaving (reduce demand charges)
- Load shifting (store off-peak energy, discharge during peak hours)
- Backup power and resilience
- Frequency regulation and grid services

**Battery Technology Comparison:**
- Lithium-ion: Most common, 90-95% round-trip efficiency, 10-15 year life
- Flow batteries: Longer duration discharge, 75-85% efficiency, 20+ year life
- Lead-acid: Lower cost, 80-85% efficiency, 5-10 year life

**Economic Considerations:**
- Current costs: $400-800 per kWh installed
- Value streams: Demand charge reduction, energy arbitrage, ancillary services
- Payback period: 5-12 years depending on application and incentives

## 8. Water and Energy Nexus

### Water Heating Efficiency

**Domestic Hot Water (DHW) Systems:**
- Water heating accounts for 5-10% of building energy in commercial facilities
- Storage tank heat loss: 1-2% per hour with typical insulation
- Recirculation pump energy: Can equal or exceed heating energy in poorly designed systems

**DHW Optimization Strategies:**
- Lower storage temperature from 140°F to 120°F (legionella risk requires periodic thermal disinfection)
- Insulate hot water tanks and first 10 feet of piping (R-12 minimum)
- Install timers on recirculation pumps (operate only when needed)
- Consider point-of-use water heaters for remote fixtures
- Use heat pump water heaters where applicable (COP of 2.5-3.5)

### Cooling Tower Water Management

**Water Conservation:**
- Cooling towers evaporate 2-3 gallons per ton-hour of cooling
- Increase cycles of concentration to reduce blowdown water
- Target 4-6 cycles with proper water treatment
- Implement conductivity-based bleed control

**Energy Implications:**
- Higher cycles of concentration reduce makeup water and associated pumping energy
- Improved water quality reduces chiller efficiency losses from fouling
- Free cooling using cooling towers when outdoor conditions permit (waterside economizer)

## 9. Behavioral and Occupant Engagement

### Energy Awareness Programs

**Communication Strategies:**
- Real-time energy dashboards in building lobbies and common areas
- Monthly energy reports highlighting consumption patterns and savings
- Goal setting and progress tracking (energy reduction targets)
- Recognition programs for departments or tenants with best performance

**Behavioral Change Initiatives:**
- "Last one out" campaigns for turning off lights and equipment
- Power management settings for computers (sleep/hibernate modes)
- Optimal thermostat use education (avoid extreme settings)
- Equipment purchasing guidelines (Energy Star requirements)

### Plug Load Management

Plug loads account for 10-30% of commercial building energy:

**Computer and IT Equipment:**
- Enable power management: Monitor sleep after 15 minutes, system sleep after 30 minutes
- Potential savings: $25-75 per computer annually
- Use laptop docking stations rather than separate desktop computers
- Consolidate and virtualize servers (reduce hardware and cooling loads)

**After-Hours Equipment:**
- Use smart power strips that cut power to peripherals when computer is off
- Unplug battery chargers when not in use (phantom loads)
- Schedule vending machine operation (compressors off overnight)
- Provide power switches for shared equipment (printers, copiers)

## 10. Measurement and Verification (M&V)

### International Performance Measurement and Verification Protocol (IPMVP)

**M&V Approaches:**
- **Option A**: Retrofit isolation with key parameter measurement
- **Option B**: Retrofit isolation with all parameter measurement
- **Option C**: Whole building analysis
- **Option D**: Calibrated simulation

**Baseline Period Requirements:**
- Minimum 12 months of pre-retrofit data
- Adjustment for non-routine events (extreme weather, operational changes)
- Documentation of baseline equipment and operational parameters
- Weather normalization using heating/cooling degree days

### Savings Calculation Methods

**Normalized Savings:**
```
Savings = Baseline Energy (adjusted) - Reporting Period Energy (adjusted) ± Adjustments
```

**Adjustment Factors:**
- Weather normalization (heating and cooling degree days)
- Occupancy changes (square footage, operating hours)
- Production levels (for industrial facilities)
- Equipment changes (additions or removals)

**Uncertainty Analysis:**
- Typical M&V uncertainty: ±5-10% of savings
- More detailed metering reduces uncertainty
- Longer measurement periods improve statistical confidence
- Document all assumptions and calculation methods

## 11. Energy Management Maintenance Practices

### Preventive Maintenance (PM) Programs

**HVAC Equipment PM Schedules:**
- **Quarterly**: Filter changes, belt inspections, temperature/pressure checks
- **Semi-annually**: Coil cleaning, lubrication, refrigerant charge verification
- **Annually**: Full system inspection, combustion analysis (boilers), vibration analysis (motors)

**Energy Impact of Poor Maintenance:**
- Dirty coils reduce heat transfer efficiency by 20-40%
- Clogged filters increase fan energy by 10-30%
- Refrigerant undercharge reduces chiller efficiency by 10-20% per 10% undercharge
- Worn belts waste 3-5% of motor power through slippage

### Retrocommissioning (RCx)

Retrocommissioning is a systematic process for identifying operational improvements:

**RCx Process Steps:**
1. Planning and data collection
2. Investigation and analysis of current operation
3. Implementation of identified measures
4. Ongoing commissioning and performance tracking

**Typical RCx Savings:**
- Energy savings: 10-20% of annual consumption
- Cost: $0.20-0.50 per square foot
- Payback period: 1-3 years
- Primary savings from HVAC scheduling, setpoint optimization, and sequence corrections

**RCx Findings (by frequency):**
- Scheduling issues: 60-80% of projects
- Sensor calibration problems: 40-60%
- Control sequence errors: 40-60%
- Equipment sizing problems: 20-40%
- Maintenance issues: 30-50%

## 12. Financial Analysis and Incentives

### Energy Project Financial Metrics

**Simple Payback Period (SPP):**
```
SPP = Project Cost / Annual Energy Savings
```
Target: Less than 3-5 years for operational measures, 5-10 years for capital projects

**Net Present Value (NPV):**
```
NPV = Sum of (Annual Savings / (1 + discount rate)^year) - Initial Investment
```
Projects with NPV > 0 are considered financially viable

**Internal Rate of Return (IRR):**
- The discount rate at which NPV = 0
- Target IRR: Greater than weighted average cost of capital (WACC) + risk premium
- Typical energy project IRR: 15-30% for operational improvements, 8-15% for capital projects

### Utility Incentive Programs

**Types of Incentives:**
- **Prescriptive rebates**: Fixed payment per unit ($/fixture, $/ton, $/HP)
- **Custom incentives**: Based on calculated savings ($/kWh saved, $/therm saved)
- **Performance incentives**: Based on measured savings verification

**Common Rebate Opportunities:**
- LED lighting: $10-50 per fixture or $0.10-0.30 per kWh saved
- HVAC equipment: $100-500 per ton for high-efficiency chillers
- VFDs: $50-150 per HP
- Building automation: $0.05-0.15 per kWh saved

**On-Bill Financing:**
- Utility provides upfront capital for energy projects
- Repayment through monthly utility bill charges
- Typically offered when savings exceed financing costs
- No upfront capital required from facility owner

## 13. Energy Procurement and Rate Optimization

### Electricity Rate Structure Analysis

**Common Rate Components:**
- **Energy charges**: $/kWh for consumption
- **Demand charges**: $/kW for peak 15-minute interval
- **Power factor penalties**: Charges for power factor below 0.95
- **Time-of-use (TOU) rates**: Higher $/kWh during peak periods

**Demand Charge Management:**
- Demand charges can represent 30-70% of commercial electricity bills
- Peak demand is typically measured as highest 15-minute average during billing period
- One brief spike sets demand charge for entire month
- Load management strategies have high ROI

**Rate Class Evaluation:**
- Verify facility is on optimal rate schedule
- Consider interruptible rates if backup generation is available
- Real-time pricing for facilities with flexible loads
- Aggregation opportunities for multiple facilities

### Power Factor Correction

**Power Factor Basics:**
- Power factor = Real Power (kW) / Apparent Power (kVA)
- Low power factor results from inductive loads (motors, transformers)
- Utilities penalize power factor below 0.90-0.95

**Correction Methods:**
- Install capacitor banks to offset inductive loads
- Size capacitors based on reactive power requirements (kVAR)
- Typical cost: $15-30 per kVAR installed
- Payback period: 1-3 years from avoided penalties

**Measures Implemented:**
1. LED lighting retrofit with occupancy sensors: 25% lighting energy reduction
2. HVAC optimization through BAS reprogramming: 20% HVAC energy reduction
3. Window film installation: 15% cooling load reduction

- Total energy reduction: 35%
- New EUI: 62 kBtu/sq.ft./year
- Annual savings: $125,000

## Conclusion

Effective energy management requires a comprehensive approach combining technology, operational practices, and continuous improvement. The strategies outlined in this guide can typically achieve 15-40% energy savings depending on facility conditions and current practices.

**Key Success Factors:**
1. **Management commitment**: Energy goals tied to organizational objectives
2. **Data-driven decisions**: Metering and monitoring to identify opportunities
3. **Systematic approach**: Address both capital improvements and operational practices
4. **Continuous improvement**: Ongoing commissioning and optimization
5. **Stakeholder engagement**: Involve occupants, operators, and management

The energy efficiency measures with the highest returns are often operational improvements requiring little or no capital investment. Start with no-cost and low-cost measures to generate savings that fund larger capital projects.

Energy management is not a one-time project but an ongoing process. Facilities that maintain active energy management programs continue to find new savings opportunities year after year, achieving cumulative savings of 30-50% or more over 5-10 years.
